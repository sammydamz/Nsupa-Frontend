Closing the loop on sachet water
A returnable, reusable alternative to single-use plastic sachets in Ghana
Team solution overview — v2, updated with mechanism detail, pouch design, and economics
1. The problem
Ghana generates roughly 1.1 million tonnes of plastic waste a year, and about 86% of it is improperly disposed of. Single-use sachet water is one of the largest contributors: Ghanaians consume an estimated 8.2 billion water sachets annually, generating around 14,000 tonnes of plastic waste a year from sachet water alone.
This is fundamentally a WASH problem wearing a plastic-waste disguise. Only about 42% of Ghanaians have access to safely managed water, so households and roadside buyers rely on sachet water as a trust workaround — and that workaround is now getting more expensive: sachet prices rose to a GH¢15 per bag cap in April 2026 due to a global polymer shortage. Discarded sachet film also clogs urban drains, a direct contributor to flooding in Accra.
2. The solution: a returnable tap-dispenser bottle
Instead of 30 single-use sachets per bag, one reusable, professionally-refilled dispenser bottle replaces the entire bag at price and volume parity, sold through the exact same roadside and traffic-hawker channel already in use today.
2.1 The mechanism, adapted from bag-in-box wine packaging
The core trick behind the bottle is borrowed directly from bag-in-box wine casks, which solve a very similar problem: how do you let liquid out gradually, over days or weeks, without air getting in and spoiling it or without the container being refilled unnoticed?
•	Inside the rigid outer shell sits a separate, flexible plastic liner — not the shell itself — that actually holds the water.
•	As water is drawn out through the tap, the liner simply collapses inward on itself. No air is ever drawn in to replace the lost volume, unlike a rigid bottle where air rushes in as liquid leaves.
•	Because the liner physically deflates rather than staying rigid, its condition tells the truth: a full liner is taut and full-volume; a used one is visibly, proportionally collapsed; and a liner that's been reopened and topped up with tap water looks abnormal — overinflated relative to how much it should have collapsed, or crinkled in the wrong way — compared to a natural factory fill.
•	This is exactly why wine boxes can sit on a shelf for weeks after opening without the wine oxidizing — the same physics here means nobody can quietly refill the liner with untreated water and pass it off as sealed.
On top of this passive mechanical honesty, we add one more layer: a breakaway tamper ring at the tap that snaps the first time it's twisted open and cannot be reset. Between the two — the liner's tell-tale collapse pattern and the ring's one-way snap — the system catches tampering without a single battery, sensor, or chip anywhere in the bottle.
2.2 Materials
Component	Material
Outer reusable shell	Transparent, light blue-tinted PET (or polycarbonate) — the same clear material used in standard 18.9L office water cooler bottles already familiar in Ghana. Chosen over opaque HDPE so buyers can visually check the liner's condition themselves before buying.
Inner sterile liner	Multilayer LDPE/EVOH laminate film, the same category of film used in bag-in-box liquid packaging
Tap/valve body	Polypropylene (PP)
One-way valve	Food-grade silicone (duckbill valve)
Tamper ring	PP or LDPE breakaway collar

3. Why it fits Ghana's existing systems
•	NASPAWAP already prices sachet water in three logistics tiers — ex-factory (GH¢8), ex-truck (GH¢10), retail cap (GH¢15) — an existing distribution chain this system rides on rather than replacing.
•	Roadside cooler-jug water vendors already exist informally; this formalizes and closes the loop on a selling pattern people already recognize.
•	Deposit-return asset models already operate in Ghana for glass beverage bottles, giving this a real local precedent.
4. The pouch: a personal reusable container
Alongside the roadside dispenser bottle, a smaller reusable pouch gives individuals a way to carry water day-to-day instead of buying a fresh sachet each time — refillable at kiosks, offices, or from the dispenser bottle itself.
Spec	Detail
Capacity	500–750ml, roughly one to one-and-a-half sachets
Material	TPU (thermoplastic polyurethane) — flexible, food-grade, and far more durable through repeated folding and washing than LDPE film, the same material family used in reusable soft flasks
Cap/valve	Wide-mouth screw cap for a fast refill motion, competitive with tearing open a sachet
Reuse life	Approximately 150 refill cycles before the material fatigues enough to retire, roughly 5–6 months of daily use
End of life	Returned at a kiosk for a deposit-back or discounted replacement, then recycled as a single material stream

The 150-cycle figure is a starting design target based on typical soft-flask material fatigue, and should be validated with the manufacturer once a prototype is in hand.
5. Economics
All figures below are illustrative planning estimates based on public sachet pricing (NASPAWAP, April 2026) and typical packaging cost ratios. They are directional, not final costings — real supplier quotes are needed to confirm unit costs before production.
5.1 The dispenser bottle vs. a bag of sachets
A bag of 30 sachets (15 liters) currently costs producers GH¢8 ex-factory, moves to GH¢10 ex-truck, and sells at a GH¢15 retail cap. Producing that bag means running 30 separate heat-seal operations and 30 separate plastic films, each with its own seal margin and failure risk.
The dispenser bottle replaces that with one shell (reused hundreds of times) and one liner per refill (one continuous film, no repeated sealing). The key cost shifts are:
•	The shell is a one-time capital cost to the producer, amortized over hundreds of refill cycles — its per-cycle cost becomes a small fraction of a pesewa once spread out.
•	One liner per 15L cycle uses less total film and far fewer sealing operations than 30 individual sachets, which should lower the per-liter packaging cost even before counting labor savings.
•	The tamper ring is a low-cost single-use component, comparable to the tamper bands already used on bottled beverage caps.
Priced at the same GH¢15 the market already accepts per 15L unit, the producer's margin should improve as packaging cost per liter falls — while the hawker and the buyer see no change in price or buying behavior at all.
5.2 The pouch: worked example
Assumptions used for this illustration: a 750ml pouch retails around GH¢4–5 to produce (roughly the packaging cost of 8–10 sachets), a refill costs the user about GH¢0.60–0.70 at a kiosk, and a single 500ml sachet currently retails around GH¢0.50.
Scenario	Cost over 150 fills
Buying sachets (750ml equivalent, 1.5 sachets per fill)	150 × 1.5 × GH¢0.50 ≈ GH¢112.50
Pouch: one-time purchase + 150 refills	GH¢4.50 + (150 × GH¢0.65) ≈ GH¢102

Even at a conservative refill price, the pouch breaks even within the first two to three weeks of normal daily use, then runs slightly cheaper than sachets for the rest of its roughly five-month life — while eliminating the plastic waste of 225 individual sachets per household over that period.
6. The software layer
All digital functionality sits with the people managing the loop — never with the bottle or the hawker, who continue to sell exactly as they do today.
•	Passive QR sticker on each shell (no power source).
•	Driver app: scans shells out for delivery and back in on return, works offline with sync.
•	Depot dashboard: tracks shell circulation, refill cycle counts, flags overdue units, and triggers MoMo payouts on confirmed returns.
•	Ordering interface: a web form plus WhatsApp and call-in intake, with three tiers — individual, office, and school — each supporting one-time or subscription (weekly/monthly) delivery.
